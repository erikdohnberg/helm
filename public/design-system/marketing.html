02
Three shapes of the same failure

No single moment is loud enough to catch the order flipping.

A one-week demo becomes the quarter.

The agreed outcome is getting the platform ready to integrate with a strategic partner. Then Sales asks for a throwaway AI demo for prospects — and it works too well. Prospects want it, sales hints it is coming, and by the time anyone looks up it has been committed for general availability, with the platform group on the hook to build it for real.

The PM assumes leadership weighed the trade. The VP assumes engineering would have flagged the date. Both assumptions are fair. Both are wrong.

The team looks busy. The number has not moved.

A migration is meant to go from fourteen of thirty-four services to done by quarter end. It freezes at fourteen for five weeks. Slack is active, threads move, standups happen on time — so the covering PM reads the only signal available from a distance, motion, and reports on track.

Nobody was holding the report against the one number that defined the outcome, so the gap stayed dark until the quarter closed on it.

No single yes was big enough to register.

Merchandising was explicitly deferred, kept off the outcome list on purpose. Over three months the growth team brings nine small asks: a banner slot, a seasonal badge, a pricing experiment, a placement test. Each is tiny. Each gets a yes, because refusing a small reasonable favour feels petty.

By August close to a fifth of the pod's capacity has gone to work everyone had agreed to postpone. There was never a decision to reprioritise — holding the running total was nobody's job.

What they have in common

Not concealment. A gap nobody owned.

A feature is legible and present. An outcome is neither, and never shows up in the room to argue for itself. The drift stays invisible because the knowledge is split across people who each assume someone else is watching the whole.

Helm is not the tool you already have.

Not a sprint plannerHelm never holds tickets, estimates or assignments. It holds what the quarter said it was for.
Not a roadmapA roadmap shows what you intend to build. A charter records what you agreed to stop building instead.
Not an OKR platformNo cascading, no grading. Three to five outcomes and the reasoning behind them.
Not a dashboardMotion is the signal that misled everyone in the second story. Helm reports the comparison, not the activity.
07

Straight answers.

Is this just an OKR tool with different words?
No cascading, no grading, no confidence scores. A quarter holds three to five outcomes with the reasoning and the trade-off attached, and the job is to notice when the work stops matching them.
Isn't this just asking PMs to say no?
The opposite. Dying on every hill is its own way to fail, and most of these asks should be absorbed. Helm's only demand is that absorbing one names what it displaces, so the trade is made by someone rather than by accumulation.
Where does the signal come from?
From the systems the work already lives in, compared against what the quarter anchored. Helm never guesses at intent — it reports what it observed and asks a person to decide. This is the part still being proved.
Who is meant to open this?
Product and engineering leadership, mostly monthly. If your team opens Helm daily, something has gone wrong with how you are using it.
08
Calibration, not a waitlist

If you've watched an agreed strategy lose to features that were never supposed to matter more, I want to hear how it went.

What was the outcome you'd signed up for? What crowded it out, and who could have seen it coming? Keep names out of it and stay vague on anything that shouldn't leave your company. The stories tell me whether I'm building against the patterns that actually break teams.
